Settings

One place for settings, usable by anyone who needs them

Building a central settings module for Ligentix, starting with Delivery Module and Shipment Approval.

Craig Wetherall, Chuka Ezeoke and Adrian · Product design lead, Product design and Language and content design

Product area
Settings
Status
In build
Timeframe
June 2026 — ongoing
Disciplines
Product design, Content design, Interaction design, Research

In the old platform (L4), settings weren’t placed with any plan. Some lived on an ad hoc page. Some were described in language only engineers could read. Some existed only in code. Even where settings were in the interface, most people who needed them couldn’t use them.

Our goal was one central place for all settings in Ligentix, usable by anyone who needs it, whether they’re an engineer, a BA or an account manager. We started with two modules, Delivery Module and Shipment Approval.

Most of this work happened before any screen was drawn. We found out what the rules actually did and who they affected. We changed what we understood the problem to be. And we made the case for a single settings module for the whole platform.

At first there wasn’t the resource to build one. We adjusted and designed settings per module and structured them to be lifted out later. However, we kept making the case, and successfully achieved a change in product direction. Because of how we’d designed it, the move cost a revision rather than a restart, with only limited front-end rework for engineering.

Delivery Module settings are in final build. Shipment Approval is in the pipeline. There is no outcome data yet. We set out below what we’ll measure.

What this work involved

This project involved:

  • defining the problem with operations, BAs and engineering
  • research through stakeholder walkthroughs
  • structuring the rules and how settings are organised
  • rewriting every rule in plain English
  • making the case for a central settings module
  • working with engineering on scope
  • testing behaviour with interactive prototypes
  • agreeing how prototypes are handed between teams

Visual design was just one part of it.

The two modules needed different things from us. Delivery Module settings already had an earlier version in L5, so that work meant merging rules that duplicated each other and capturing information earlier. Shipment Approval was closer to starting over, against rules that nest three levels deep and configuration that existed only in code. The same approach had to cover both.

Rules written as plain-language statements worked on Delivery Module, held in the Shipment Approval panel, and needed a different approach on the Shipment Approval rules table, which is where we moved to sentence based rows within the tables.

1. Context: why this mattered

The business problem

Settings slowed down onboarding, took engineering time and carried a real risk of costly mistakes.

L4 grew by addition. When a client wanted something slightly different, a new rule appeared next to a near-identical one. After years of this, the Shipment Approval side alone had roughly 50 to 60 rules, and nobody could tell us what all of them did.

Some configuration wasn’t in the interface at all. It was in the code. A client request became an engineering ticket, and someone had to read the source code to find out what the system currently did.

Onboarding a customer and configuring their settings were close to the same job, done twice, in two places that didn’t connect. That puts settings on the critical path for how quickly Ligentia can bring a customer live, and allow us to advocate for a similar approach for onboarding.

Who it affected

The problem wasn’t only for the people configuring settings. It reached BAs, account managers, engineers and, in the end, customers.

When the BAs were asked about specific rules, the answer was usually the same: “We don’t know what that is” or “we don’t touch that.” Using the system depended on knowledge picked up over time, not on anything the screen explained.

BAs and account managers were continually chasing information before and after the customer was initially set up.

Engineers had effectively become the support queue for settings, which used capacity that should have been better utilised elsewhere.

What we found

What we learned

These settings aren’t preferences. They are instructions for a physical operation, and a wrong setting shows up later as a real cost on a real shipment.

This came from screen-shares with Dennis Jones, who went through the L4 rules one at a time and explained what each one does. A clear example was rent start dates. The same setting behaves differently depending on the customer’s country. If it’s set wrongly, someone is invoiced materially more or less than they should be.

That changed what the screen had to be. It couldn’t be a preferences panel. It had to be closer to an instrument that you can’t set wrongly by accident.

Our sources were qualitative. Most of our understanding came from stakeholder walkthroughs:

  • Dennis, on what the rules do in operations
  • Timea Toth, provided additional insight into L4 settings
  • engineering, on what made the area hard to maintain

We also read the existing screens closely. We did use some figures, such as how many suppliers a customer can hold and the migration backlog. We didn’t use support tickets, because the old tool was internal and run by engineers.

2. Approach: what we did

The case for one settings module

We made the case that one central settings module was the right route, both tactically and strategically. At first there wasn’t the resource to build it. So we designed per module, structured to be lifted out later, and kept making the case. A central module is now the agreed direction. Because of how we’d designed it, the move cost the design team a revision rather than a restart, and the engineering rework was limited, mostly front end.

Our case was focused on a longer term vision for platform settings, not any one screen. Someone who has just set up a customer and needs to change their configuration shouldn’t have to know which module owns which setting. One place to go means one thing to learn, and one structure to maintain. Settings collected via an extensive onboarding process should not need re-creating or re-entering.

At the start, engineering supported the idea, but had limited resource available. That was a fair call at the time. So we designed settings per module, and made every structural choice assuming they would later be combined. Because that strategic thinking had already been laid out, this had the benefit of reducing future product and design work whilst providing engineering with additional context they could consider during build.

We did, however, keep making the case. Over the course of the project, design explained why a central module was the better route, for users now and for the platform long term. It took sustained effort but Design successfully convinced senior product stakeholders that this was the right route. With engineering’s agreement, a central module was adopted as the approved direction.

To support this advocacy, the design team had built a proof of concept that put known L4 rules into the design system (SwiftDS), and adjusted them to support a modular structure. This was an asset intended to help teams align on a long term vision and experience the benefits of this approach directly. An example of where this approach would directly avoid costly future rework was in exposing the need for three level deep rule navigation. It showed the default design system tab approach would cause distinct usability issues. Content was visually competing with each other in the same space, confusing users. We required an alternative, long term approach.

We built those early explorations to work either per module or standalone, so when the decision changed, the design was ready for it.

Key decisions

Each decision below shows the problem, who was involved, what we decided and the effect we expect.

Fewer rules, and the right information captured earlier · Delivery Module

Problem
Rules in the first version of Delivery Module settings looked independent, but several did the same job under different names. L4's sprawl had copied itself into the new platform. Information that should have been captured at customer setup was being chased later.
Who
The design team, and the Delivery Module BAs.
Decision
We merged rules that duplicated each other, deleted repeats, and added fields at setup for information that was previously chased mid-process.
Expected effect
Fewer rules that are easier to read. Fewer and shorter tickets for account managers.

Copy a client’s configuration · Delivery Module first, reused on Shipment Approval

Problem
Clients often need a configuration that differs from an existing one in only a few places, but it had to be rebuilt rule by rule.
Who
The design team and Dennis Jones.
Decision
We let users duplicate a client's existing configuration and then change what's different.
Expected effect
Faster setup for any client whose configuration is close to one that already exists.

After seeing it work effectively on Delivery Module, Dennis asked for it across all the Shipment Approval tabs. That is our clearest early sign that it solved a real problem.

Parameters first, then toggles · Shipment Approval

Problem
The early Shipment Approval structure placed a rule's parameters under First Approval. A client who wanted Highlight Validation without auto-approval couldn't get it cleanly. Anyone who wanted both had to define the same parameters twice.
Who
Jonathan Maynard found the flaw. The design team reworked the model.
Decision
Each rule and its parameters are defined once. First Approval, Highlight Validation and Review Hold sit on top as independent toggles.
Expected effect
A simpler, more efficient model with no duplicate setup, and fewer ways to configure a client incorrectly, reducing errors.

Sentences instead of columns · Shipment Approval (pattern first used on Delivery Module)

Problem
Rule creation in the SIP (slide in panel) already used plain language, so setting up a rule read like writing a statement. That worked on Delivery Module. It was well validated. Shipment Approval rules nest three levels deep, and the table carried a separate column for every parameter any rule type might use. The pattern held in the SIP but not in the table.
Scale of it
Eleven columns, six of them parameters that only apply to some rule types, so most cells were empty. Reading one rule meant scanning the full width of the table to find the two or three values that applied to it, and the rule name on its own did not say what the rule checked. The table was also longer than it needed to be, so finding a rule required extensive scrolling.
Who
The design team.
Decision
Each rule in the table is written as a short sentence instead of a row of yes/no columns. The easy choice was to accept the sparse grid. We decided not to.
Expected effect
The six parameter columns collapse into one that reads as a sentence, taking the table from eleven columns to six. A rule can be read without opening it, and each row can expand to explain what the rule checks.

Logs moved out of the rule · Shipment Approval

Problem
Dennis flagged audit logging as critical. A rule can be deleted, but its history still has to survive, so logs couldn't live inside the rule. Logs were also spread across notification alerts and migrated clients.
Who
Dennis Jones and the design team.
Decision
We moved logs out of the SIP to one top-level tab that can be filtered by rule, by deletion and by history. Later, when someone suggested copying the existing logs across as they were, we explained that log content differs by settings area, so a single view needs designing rather than copying.
Expected effect
Audit history survives rule deletion, and logs can be found in one place.

Clashing rules are disabled, not hidden · Shipment Approval

Problem
Some rules can't be used together. Our first idea was to filter the available rules by customer.
Who
The design team proposed the filtering approach. Dennis Jones and Jonathan Maynard pushed back on it.
Decision
Choosing one rule greys out the rules that clash with it, and explains why. We dropped filtering by customer, because predefining which rules each customer can have does not scale.
Expected effect
Users can't create conflicting setups, and the system doesn't need rework each time a new customer type arrives.

Language as its own workstream

The language problem was big enough to need someone’s full attention, so we split the work. One designer led language while the rest of the team led the interaction design.

We set a voice rather than checking every line of text one at a time. That meant plain English, as little jargon as possible, rules written as sentences, and an example wherever a rule needed one. One early draft came out too warm and was pulled back toward plain instruction.

We tested the tone with Dee Williams, and checked accuracy with Dennis and the BAs.

How we worked

Decision

We tested behaviour with interactive prototypes instead of static screens, and the quality of feedback improved. We also agreed with other teams how prototypes should be handed over.

The design team built and iterated on prototypes made with Claude, so they behaved more like the real product than traditional prototypes do. In reviews with BAs, engineering and the product owner, people could click through and interact with how the screens actually worked instead of imagining it. Feedback became more focused and complete, and decisions came out of those sessions. One example is replacing a dropdown with a strict search, because the backend structure made the dropdown the wrong choice.

AI prototyping tools were new to several teams during this project. Prototypes produced outside design helped show intent, but they mixed in code-level and BA-level detail, which made the scope look bigger than it was and caused unexpected friction between teams. We read them down to the core flows. We then worked with other teams to agree what a prototype should contain at handover and evolved cross team processes to solve the issues caused by new ways of working.

3. Value: what design contributed

Design shaped what was built, not only how it looked.

  • We redefined the problem. Settings were treated as a source of operational risk, not as preferences.
  • We saw the structural problem coming. A design proof of concept found it before the project started.
  • We made the case for a central module. It was sustained over the project, and it is now the agreed direction for the platform.
  • We kept the better option affordable. Designing per module, structured to be lifted out, meant the change of direction cost a revision rather than a restart.
  • We reduced the work. Rules were merged, information is captured earlier, and configurations can be copied.
  • We made the rules readable. Language was handled as its own workstream.
  • We improved how teams work together. Interactive prototypes led to better review sessions, and handover between teams was agreed.

Where it stands and how we’ll measure it

Delivery Module settings are in final build. Shipment Approval is in progress. There is no outcome data yet.

Once Delivery Module is live, we want evidence of someone’s job getting easier. For example, an account manager or BA making a change they would previously have raised a ticket for.

4. Forward: how design gets involved

What this project shows about involving design early

What we learned

The most valuable design work on this project happened before anything was built. Bringing design in early on platform-wide decisions gives that work the most room to pay off.

Several of the outcomes in this case study came from design being involved early:

  • A design proof of concept found the structural problem before the project properly started.
  • Designing per module, structured to be lifted out later, kept the central module affordable when the direction changed.
  • Screen-shares with Dennis changed what the problem was before any screens were drawn.

Each of these was cheap because it happened early. The same insight found later, after build, would have meant rework.

Platform-wide decisions like this one affect how every module works, and how everyone who uses Ligentix finds their way around it. They benefit most from design involvement at the point where the direction is being set, not after it’s been decided.

Where this applies next

Two open questions are good examples of where early design involvement would help.

Notification settings. Every module has them. It isn’t clear that someone changing notifications thinks in modules at all, or whether they’d expect one notifications page. That’s a platform-wide structural question of the same kind as the central settings module, and it’s at the stage where design input costs least.

Testing with people who don’t know the domain. We haven’t yet checked whether someone new to the area can read a sentence-based rule and understand it. Testing this before Shipment Approval is built is cheaper than changing it afterwards.
